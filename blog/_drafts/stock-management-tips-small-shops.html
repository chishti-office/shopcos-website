10 Stock Management Tips for Small Shops in Pakistan

October 14, 20267 min readInventory

Every shopkeeper in Pakistan knows the two worst feelings in business. The first: a customer asks for an item and you have to say "khatam ho gaya hai" — watching them walk to your competitor. The second: opening your storeroom and finding cartons of stock that nobody is buying, your cash locked up in dead inventory. Both problems have the same root cause: poor stock management.

Studies of small retail businesses consistently show that inventory is where most of a shop's working capital sits — often 60–70% of it. Manage it well and your cash flows freely. Manage it badly and your money gathers dust on shelves. Here are ten practical tips that work in real Pakistani shops, from Lahore's busy bazaars to small-town kiryana stores.

1. Set a Reorder Level for Every Item

Do not wait until a shelf is empty to order. For each product, decide a minimum quantity that triggers a new order. A simple formula: reorder level = average daily sales × supplier delivery days + safety buffer.

Example: you sell 10 packs of tea daily, your supplier delivers in 3 days. Reorder level = 10 × 3 + 20 (buffer) = 50 packs. When stock hits 50, you order. No guessing, no emergencies.

2. Follow FIFO — First In, First Out

Always sell the oldest stock first. New cartons go behind, old cartons come forward. This single habit prevents most expiry losses in kiryana stores and pharmacies. Train your staff on it: when restocking shelves, the new items go at the back. It takes five extra seconds and saves thousands of rupees.

3. Identify Your Fast and Slow Movers

Not all products are equal. In a typical shop:

  • Fast movers (20% of items): generate 80% of sales — cooking oil, sugar, tea, milk. Never let these run out. Keep deeper stock.
  • Slow movers: sell occasionally — specialty items, seasonal goods. Keep minimal stock, order only when needed.

Review your sales every month and adjust. Many shopkeepers are shocked to discover they are overstocking slow items while their bestsellers keep running out.

4. Compare Suppliers — Don't Marry One

Loyalty to a supplier is good; blindness is expensive. Every quarter, compare purchase rates from at least 2–3 suppliers for your top 20 items. Even a Rs 5 difference per unit on an item you sell 500 units of monthly is Rs 2,500 saved every month — Rs 30,000 a year from one phone call.

5. Stock Seasonally, Not Emotionally

Pakistani retail is deeply seasonal. Stock up before Ramzan (dates, cooking oil, beverages), before Eid (clothes, shoes, gifts), and before winter (tea, dry fruits, heating items). But buy based on last year's actual sales, not optimism. If you sold 200 kg of dates last Ramzan, ordering 500 kg this year is not ambition — it is a gamble with your cash.

6. Clear Dead Stock Ruthlessly

Every shop accumulates items that do not sell. Set a rule: anything that has not sold a single unit in 90 days gets action — discount it, bundle it with a fast mover ("buy 2 soaps, get this free"), or return it to the supplier. Dead stock is not an asset; it is frozen cash occupying shelf space that a profitable item could use.

7. Do a Physical Count Every Month

Your records say 100 units; the shelf has 87. That gap of 13 units is telling you something — theft, damage, unrecorded sales, or supplier short-delivery. A monthly physical count of your top 50 items takes about an hour and catches problems while they are small. Shops that count monthly lose far less to "mysterious" shrinkage than shops that count yearly.

8. Record Every Purchase the Day It Arrives

Stock that is not recorded does not exist in your system. When supplier goods arrive, enter them the same day: item, quantity, purchase rate, and batch/expiry if applicable. This gives you two superpowers: accurate profit calculation (you know your real buying cost) and the ability to spot supplier billing errors immediately.

9. Track Expiry Dates Proactively

For pharmacies and kiryana stores, expired products are pure loss. Maintain a simple list sorted by expiry date, and check it monthly. Items expiring within 60 days should be: pushed to the front of shelves, offered at a small discount, or returned to the supplier (many Pakistani distributors accept near-expiry returns). Every expired pack of medicine thrown away is money straight into the dustbin.

10. Let Data Predict, Don't Just React

The best shopkeepers develop a feel for demand over years. But you do not need decades of experience if your system tracks sales history. Look at the last 30 days of sales for each item — that is your demand forecast. If you sold 300 units last month, ordering 320–350 for next month is a data-driven decision, not a guess.

Real example: A pharmacy owner in Karachi started tracking reorder levels and expiry dates digitally. In six months, his stock-outs dropped by 70%, expired medicine losses fell from Rs 18,000/month to under Rs 3,000/month, and he freed up Rs 200,000 in cash previously locked in overstocked slow movers.

The Bottom Line

Good stock management is not complicated — it is consistent. Set reorder levels, follow FIFO, know your fast movers, count monthly, and act on data instead of gut feeling. Start with just two or three of these tips this week. Within a month, you will see the difference in your cash flow, your shelves, and your peace of mind.
