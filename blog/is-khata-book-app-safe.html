Picture a shopkeeper in Samundri who has kept his udhar in a phone app for three years. Two hundred customers, every purchase and every payment recorded, the whole history of who owes what sitting in one small rectangle of glass and plastic. One evening his phone slips out of his pocket at the bus stop. By the time he goes back, it is gone — and with it, three years of khata. He remembers the big names, roughly. He does not remember the Rs 2,400 that Ahmed owes from March, or the Rs 1,150 that the tailor paid in two instalments, or whether the flour shop next door settled last month or not. He cannot prove a single balance. Within a week, three customers have quietly stopped paying, because they know he cannot prove what they owe.

This is the question every shopkeeper should ask before moving his khata into an app: is a khata book app safe — and what actually happens if your data is lost? The honest answer is that the app itself is rarely the problem. The problem is what happens around the app: the phone, the account, the backup that nobody ever checked. This guide walks through the four ways shops actually lose their khata data, what “safe” really means, the five questions that separate a trustworthy app from a risky one, and a sixty-second test you can run today on whatever app you already use.

The 4 Ways a Shop Loses Its Khata Data

Data loss almost never looks like a hacker in a film. It looks ordinary, and it happens on ordinary days. Here are the four ways it actually happens to Pakistani shops:

1. The phone is stolen or lost

This is the most common one, and the most final. A phone left on the counter while you serve a customer, a pocket picked in a crowded bazaar, a phone forgotten in a rickshaw — and the khata goes with it. A paper register can be stolen too, but a thief steals a phone to sell the phone; he does not care that your life's work was inside it, and he will wipe it within the hour. If the khata lived only on that phone, and there was no backup anywhere else, the record is gone the moment the phone is wiped. No shopkeeper can argue with a customer from memory, and memory is exactly what a thief leaves you with.

2. The phone dies — screen, battery, water, heat

Phones die in ways that have nothing to do with software. A cracked screen that no longer responds to touch. A battery that swells in the summer heat. A phone dropped in a bucket of water during shop cleaning, or left on a counter in the rain. The data is usually still sitting on the phone's storage chip — but you cannot reach it, because the screen is dead or the phone will not switch on, and getting the chip read by a repair shop costs thousands and often fails anyway. For a shopkeeper, a dead phone and a lost phone feel exactly the same: the khata is unreachable, and the shop still has to open tomorrow.

3. The app is deleted, the phone is reset, the account is locked

Sometimes the shopkeeper does it to himself. A child plays with the phone and deletes the app. A phone is handed to a repair shop for a software problem, and it comes back factory-reset. A new phone is bought, the old one is sold, and nobody remembers to move the data first. Or the app account itself is the problem: the app is tied to a phone number, the SIM is lost or the number changes, and the one-time password goes to a number that no longer exists — so the account, and everything in it, is locked behind a door whose key was a SIM card. None of these need a thief. They need one careless afternoon.

4. The data is there, but you cannot reach it

This is the quietest kind of loss, and the one shopkeepers discover too late. The app works, the data is in it, but there is no export button, no backup file, and no way to move the record anywhere else. Then the app company shuts down, or stops updating the app, or starts charging a monthly fee you cannot pay, or the app simply stops working on your new phone — and your khata is trapped inside a dead product. A record you cannot take with you is not your record. It is a loan from a company that can take it back whenever it likes.

Notice what all four have in common: in every case, the shopkeeper did nothing wrong on the day it happened. The mistake was made months earlier, on the day he chose an app without asking where the second copy of his data lives. That is the whole lesson of this article, and it is worth saying plainly: a khata record with no second copy is not a record. It is a hope.

“Safe” Means Three Different Things

When a shopkeeper asks “is this app safe?”, he is usually asking three different questions at once, and a good answer has to cover all three. An app can pass one and fail the other two, so check them separately:

  • Safe from loss. If the phone is stolen, broken or reset tonight, can you get your khata back tomorrow? This is the backup question, and it is the one that matters most. A “yes” here means there is a real second copy — a backup file, a cloud copy, or the same data on your shop computer — that you have actually tested, not just been told about.
  • Safe from other eyes. Who else can see your customers' names and balances? A khata is private business: it says who is in debt, how much, and for how long. If the app stores your data on someone else's server, that company's staff, its advertisers, or anyone who breaks into its systems can potentially see it. If the app is free, ask what it sells — because if you are not paying for the app, your data is often the product. An app that keeps your data on your own phone or your own shop PC is private by design; an app that uploads everything to a server is private only by promise.
  • Safe from lock-in. Can you leave? If you stop using the app tomorrow, can you take every customer, every balance and every transaction with you, in a file you can open yourself? An app that lets you export your full history is a tool you own. An app that does not is a cage with a friendly colour scheme. Before you put years of udhar into any app, find the export button. If there is none, that is your answer.

Most khata apps score well on the first question in their advertising and badly on the second and third in their fine print. That is why the right test is not “does this app look safe?” but “where exactly does my data live, who else can see it, and how do I take it home?”

The number to remember: take your own shop and do this sum — your number of udhar customers, multiplied by the average balance each one owes. A shop with 200 udhar customers at an average of Rs 3,000 each is carrying Rs 600,000 of credit on a single phone. Six lakh rupees of trust, sitting in a device that can be stolen in ten seconds, dropped in a bucket of water, or wiped by a child. That number, not the price of the phone, is what is actually at stake when a khata app has no backup.

The Paper Register vs the Phone App: Which Loses Data Faster?

It is tempting to say the phone app is obviously safer than the paper register. It is not that simple. Each one loses data in its own way, and an honest comparison helps you keep the best of both:

Destroyed by fire or flood?Yes — one copy, gone foreverNo — the phone usually survives a shop fire in a pocket
Stolen?Rarely targeted; a thief wants cash, not a ledgerYes — the phone is the target, the khata is collateral damage
One mistake erases everything?A torn page loses one customer; the rest survivesOne reset or one deleted app can erase every customer at once
Can you make a second copy?Only by rewriting it by hand — nobody doesEasy — if the app has backup or export; impossible if it does not
Who else can read it?Only someone holding the bookAnyone with the phone, the account, or the company's server
Needs electricity or internet?NeverNeeds a charged phone; backup needs internet or a PC
Finds a customer's balance fast?Slow — flip pages, add by handInstant — search the name, balance on screen

The paper register's strength is that it fails slowly and locally: one torn page, one faded entry. The phone app's strength is that it can be copied perfectly in seconds. But the phone app's weakness is that it can fail completely and instantly — everything, all at once, with no warning. That is why the answer is not to pick a side. It is to use the app and keep a second copy somewhere the phone is not: a backup on your shop computer, an export file on a USB drive, a monthly printout in a drawer. The shops that never lose their khata are not the ones with the fanciest app. They are the ones with two copies.

5 Questions to Ask Before You Trust Any Khata App

Before you move your udhar into any app — free or paid, famous or new — ask these five questions. A trustworthy app answers all five clearly. An app that dodges them is telling you something:

1. Where does my data live — my phone, my PC, or someone else's server?

If the answer is “on our cloud”, ask whose cloud, in which country, and what happens to your data if you stop paying. If the answer is “on your own device”, ask what happens when that device is stolen. The safest answer for a shop is data on your own shop computer, with a backup copy somewhere you control. Your shop's records should live in your shop, not in a stranger's data centre.

2. Is there an automatic backup — and where does it go?

A backup you have to remember to make is a backup that will not exist on the day you need it. Look for automatic daily backup, and find out where the backup file actually goes: your own Google Drive, your own computer, a USB stick — or the company's server, where it is only as safe as the company's business. Automatic is good. Automatic to a place you control is better.

3. Can I export everything — customers, balances, full history — to a file I own?

Open the app now and look for an export or download option. Can you get a file — a spreadsheet, a PDF, a database copy — that lists every customer and every transaction? Can you open that file on another computer without the app? If the app only lets you see your data inside the app, you do not own your data. You are renting the sight of it.

4. What happens if I forget my password, lose my number, or change my SIM?

Most khata apps log you in with a phone number and a one-time code. That means your entire khata is chained to one SIM card. If the SIM is lost, stolen, or the number changes, the code goes nowhere and the account is locked. Ask the app what the recovery path is: an email login, a recovery code you can print and keep, a support team that actually answers. If the only answer is “use the same number”, then your khata is one lost SIM away from being unreachable.

5. What happens if the company closes, stops the app, or doubles the price?

Free apps close. Small app companies get bought, run out of money, or simply stop updating. When that happens, an app that stores your data on its own servers can vanish overnight — and with it, your khata. Ask whether the app works offline, whether your data stays usable without the company's servers, and whether a paid version is a one-time purchase or a monthly charge that can rise whenever the company likes. A one-time payment for software that runs on your own PC does not have this problem at all: the company can disappear and your shop keeps running.

If an app cannot answer these five questions in plain words, it has not earned your khata — no matter how many downloads it has or how pretty its screens are. The 60-Second Test: Can You Get Your Data Back Right Now?

You do not need a stolen phone to find out whether your khata is safe. Run this test today, on whatever app you already use. It takes sixty seconds, and it tells you the truth:

  1. Find the backup date. Open the app's settings and look for the last backup. If it says “last backup: today”, good. If it says “last backup: never” or there is no backup setting at all, your khata has no second copy — fix that today, before anything else.
  2. Export your data. Use the app's export or download option and save the file somewhere outside the phone — your email, your Google Drive, your shop computer. Then open the file and check it: are all your customers there, with the right balances? A backup you have never opened is a backup you cannot trust.
  3. Ask the 2 a.m. question. Imagine your phone is stolen tonight. Tomorrow morning, how do you open the shop and know who owes what? If the answer is “install the app on another phone and log in” — try it now, on a family member's phone, and see if it actually works. If the answer is “I don't know”, then tonight is the night to make a second copy.

If your app passes all three steps, your khata is genuinely safe, and you can keep using it with confidence. If it fails any one of them, do not wait for a bad day to teach you the lesson — make the second copy now, while the data is still in front of you. And if you cannot find a backup or export option at all, that is not a small missing feature. It is the app telling you, in its own way, that your data was never really yours.
