Walk into any darzi's shop in Pakistan and you will find the same thing on the counter: a small register, its pages full of names, numbers and dates in hurried handwriting. That register is the naap book, and it is the whole business. Every customer's chest, length, shoulder and sleeve. Every suit's style — the collar, the cuffs, the pockets. Every advance paid and every rupee still owed. Every delivery date promised. A kiryana shop can lose its cash box and still open tomorrow. A tailor who loses his naap book loses the measurements of fifty customers and the proof of the money they already paid.

And yet the naap book is still, in most tailor shops, a paper register with no copy, no backup and no search. This guide explains what tailor shop software actually does for a darzi — not a generic shop, a tailor — which features are worth paying for, what the switch honestly costs, and how to move from the register to a screen without stopping work for a single day. If you stitch shalwar kameez, kurtas, suits or waistcoats for a living, this is written for you.

The Naap Book Is the Whole Business

To understand why tailor software exists, first understand what the naap book really holds. It is not one list — it is four records squeezed into one register:

  • The measurements. Chest, waist, hips, shoulder, sleeve length, collar, shirt length, shalwar length and more — different for every customer, different again for every garment type. A shalwar kameez naap is not a suit naap, and a waistcoat needs its own set. Get one number wrong and the suit does not fit.
  • The style notes. "Collar band wala", "cuff plain", "do jeb, ek andar wali", "shalwar narrow". These little notes are the difference between the suit the customer imagined and a suit he will argue about.
  • The money. The advance (bayana) paid on order, the balance due on delivery, and — for regular customers — the running udhar that stretches across months.
  • The dates. When the suit was ordered, when it was promised, and which karigar is stitching it. In Eid season a busy bazaar tailor takes 10 to 20 orders a day, and at the peak there can be 300 suits in the pipeline at once.

When all four records live in one paper book, the book becomes the shop. The ustad knows which page is whose; the karigar waits for the ustad to read it out; the customer trusts that the entry is there. That works — right up until the day it does not.

What Goes Wrong With a Paper Naap Book

Ask any tailor who has been in business ten years and he will have at least one of these stories. Most have all of them:

  • The book gets lost or damaged. A register left on a counter is one spilled cup of chai, one monsoon leak, one curious child away from being unreadable. There is no second copy. When the book goes, the measurements of every pending customer go with it — and you cannot re-measure a customer who has gone back to Lahore.
  • Only one person can read it. The ustad's handwriting makes sense to the ustad. The karigar squints at a "38" that might be a "33", cuts the cloth, and the suit comes back for alteration — or the customer never comes back at all. A wrong number wastes fabric, time and a customer.
  • Finding anything takes minutes. "Ahmed sahab ka suit kahan hai?" means flipping forty pages while the customer stands at the counter. Multiply that by twenty customers a day and the shop spends an hour a day just searching its own book.
  • Advances get disputed. The customer says he paid Rs 2,000; the book says Rs 1,000 — or the entry is missing entirely because the order was taken in a rush. Now it is your word against his, at the counter, in front of other customers. There is no receipt to settle it, because the book is the receipt.
  • Delivery dates slip silently. A paper book never reminds you. The suit promised for Friday sits unstitched because nobody checked the page, and the customer arrives to find it is not ready. He tells three people. In a bazaar, that is advertising you pay for with your reputation.
  • There is no backup. Fire, theft, a burst pipe — a paper record has exactly one copy, and it sits in the shop. A tailor's whole customer history can end in one bad night.

None of this means the naap book is a bad idea. It means the naap book is a good idea trapped in a fragile format. The measurements, the advances, the dates — all of it is exactly what a computer is good at keeping.

What Tailor Shop Software Actually Does

Tailor shop software is not a kiryana billing program with the word "tailor" on the box. A proper one does five jobs that map directly onto the naap book:

  • A digital naap book. Every customer gets a profile with their measurements saved — chest, length, shoulder, sleeve, collar — separately for shalwar kameez, kurta, suit and waistcoat. When a regular customer orders again, there is no re-measuring. His naap is already there, exactly as it was last time, and you can adjust one number instead of starting from zero.
  • Order tracking from cutting to ready. Each order carries a status: cutting, stitching, pressing, ready. You can find any order in seconds by the customer's name or phone number — no page-flipping — and every morning you can see exactly which suits are due today.
  • Advance and balance, recorded properly. Every payment is entered against the order, the balance is calculated automatically, and the customer gets a bill on WhatsApp. The "maine kitne diye thay" argument ends, because both sides have the same record on their phones. Our guide on how to send a bill on WhatsApp shows how that works at the counter.
  • Karigar accounts. Which karigar stitched which suit, at what piece rate, and how much he is owed this week. No more remembering who did what, and no more paying the wrong man for someone else's work.
  • Delivery reminders and ready messages. The software knows what is due today and what is late, and it can message the customer the moment his suit is ready — instead of him arriving to ask.

Notice what is missing from that list: nothing about barcodes, shelf labels or expiry dates. A tailor does not need a supermarket's software. He needs his naap book, his karigar hisab and his delivery dates — on a screen, searchable, backed up, and readable by anyone in the shop.

The 8 Features That Matter for a Tailor

When you compare tailor software, ignore the long feature lists and check these eight. If a program cannot do them, it is a general shop program wearing a tailor's name:

  1. Saved measurement profiles, by garment type. A shalwar kameez naap, a kurta naap, a suit naap and a waistcoat naap are four different sets of numbers. The software should keep them separately under one customer, and let you copy last time's naap into a new order with one click.
  2. An order status pipeline. Cutting → stitching → pressing → ready. If the software only knows "ordered" and "delivered", it cannot tell you what is actually happening in the shop today.
  3. Advance and balance per order. Not just a total — the advance paid, the balance due, and the payment history, order by order, so a customer can never dispute what he paid.
  4. Karigar piece-rate accounts. Each karigar's completed suits, his rate per suit, and what he is owed. The ustad should be able to settle a week's karigar payments in minutes, from the record, not from memory.
  5. Delivery-date alerts. A daily list of what is due today and what is already late. This one feature alone saves more customers than any other — a suit delivered on the promised day is a customer who comes back.
  6. WhatsApp bills and ready messages. The bill, the balance and the "suit tayyar hai" message should go to the customer's phone in one tap. A paper bill fades; a WhatsApp bill is still there when he comes to collect.
  7. Fabric stock in metres and thaan. A tailor buys cloth by the thaan and sells it by the metre. The software should track fabric the way a tailor buys it — metres remaining, not "units" — and warn you before the popular colours run out in Eid season.
  8. Full offline working, with a backup. Load shedding is a fact of life in a Pakistani bazaar. The software must bill, search and record with no internet at all, keep the data on your own PC, and back it up automatically every day. If your data lives only on someone else's server, it is not your data.

A program that does all eight is a tailor's program. A program that does three of them and calls the rest "coming soon" is a bill you will pay while you keep using the register anyway.

Six Questions to Ask Before You Buy

Before you pay for any tailor software, ask the seller these six questions — and be suspicious of any answer that is vague:

  1. "Does it have tailor fields, or is it a general shop program?" Ask to see the measurement screen. If there is no place to save a customer's naap by garment type, it is not tailor software, whatever the name on the box says.
  2. "Does it work fully without internet?" Turn off the Wi-Fi in the demo and try to take an order. If it stops, it will stop in your shop too, every time the light goes.
  3. "Where is my data, and can I back it up myself?" The right answer is "on your own PC, with a daily automatic backup you can copy to a USB". The wrong answer is a shrug, or "it is in the cloud, don't worry".
  4. "Can it send the bill and the ready message on WhatsApp?" Your customers live on WhatsApp. A bill they can see on their phone ends more arguments than any feature in the program.
  5. "Can the karigar see only his own work?" Your karigar needs to see his suits and his payment — not your full khata, not other karigars' rates, not the shop's profit. A staff view that hides the rest is not a luxury; it is how a shop with three karigars stays peaceful.
  6. "One-time or monthly — what does three years cost?" Make them say the number out loud. Then compare it with the table above before you decide.

Switching Without Stopping Work

The fear that stops most tailors from switching is simple: "If I change the system, the shop stops." It does not have to. The tailors who switch cleanly all do it the same way — in parallel, for two weeks:

  • Week one: run both. Keep writing in the naap book, and enter the same orders in the software. It takes an extra minute per order, and it means the shop never depends on a system nobody has learned yet.
  • Enter pending orders first. Start with the suits that are due soonest, not the oldest pages. The orders that will be collected this week are the ones you need searchable first.
  • Photograph the old pages. Before the old registers fade or get lost, photograph every page with your phone and keep the photos in a folder. It is a free backup, and it takes an evening.
  • Teach the karigar only his part. He does not need the whole system. He needs to see his suits, mark them done, and check his payment. Ten minutes of training, not a training course.
  • Let the first Eid prove it. The real test is the rush: search a name instead of flipping pages, message ten customers that their suits are ready, and settle a week's karigar payments from the record. After one Eid on the software, nobody in the shop will ask for the register back.

The switch is not a project. It is two weeks of doing both, and then the book retires to the shelf — where it belongs, as a memory, not as the business.

The Bottom Line

A customer judges a tailor on two things: does the suit fit, and was it ready on the day he was promised. The fitting is your skill, and no software can stitch for you. But the promise — the date, the advance, the measurement, the karigar who is doing it — that is a record, and a record belongs on a screen that can be searched, backed up and read by anyone in the shop.

The naap book served tailors for generations, and it deserves its place on the shelf. But the business it holds — the measurements, the money, the dates — is worth more than one paper register in one shop. Put it where it cannot be lost, and the next time a customer asks "mera suit tayyar hai?", the answer takes five seconds, not five minutes of page-flipping.
